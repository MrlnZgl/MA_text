\chapter{Results}


In this chapter the results of the executed simulations are presented and their relevance discussed.
First the simulations are verified through a comparison with the results of the plane \acrshort{tbl} simulations.
Then the results of the three simulated cases R100deg30, R500deg18 and R100deg90 are analysed each comparing its base simulations with the optimised version.
Finally, the optimised simulations of the three cases are compared with each other.

\section{Plane section verification} \label{sec:planeTBLVerif}
The following section works as a reliability test of the governed results for the curved \acrshort{tbl} simulations.
As described in XX we detected pressure flcutuations in the snapshots during the simulations.
To ensure that these flcutuations leave the long-term statistics unaffected we verified the statistics of the curved \acrshort{tbl} simulations in the straight inlet.
In order to do this we compare the statistics of case R100deg30 in optimised and base configuration with the ones from the plane \acrfull{tbl} setup and the already introduced reference data for plane \acrshort{tbl} simulations from XX.
The statistics are taken at $\mathrm{Re}_{\theta} = 700$ in all performed simulations, which corresponds to a position of around $s_{Re_{\theta}= 700} = \frac{1}{2} s_p$.
Therefore, the impact of the curvature should be negligible at this position and all setups should show similar behaviour.
Following, the results of this comparison are presented for case R100deg30. 
In order to circumvent the presentation of repetitive results, analogous analysis for the remaining curved configurations are not shown here.

The first and second order statistics of the compared simulations are depicted in \autoref{fig:inflowSecVerif} (a) - (c).
The streamwise velocity profile in viscous scaling is shown in \autoref{fig:planeTBLverifU}.
It can be seen, that all profile match each other. 
As already discussed in \autoref{subsec:planeTBLResults} only the free-stream velocity of the reference data is slightly below the other profiles since they are reported at a lower value of $\mathrm{Re}_{\theta}$.
Therefore, the boundary layer thickness is smaller which corresponds to a faster achievement of the free-stream velocity.
In \autoref{fig:inflowSecVerifFluct} the Reynolds tress are visualised. 
We can observe a good fit of the performed simulations for all components.
Again, the reference data show a small difference in the outer region of the boundary layer, respectively higher $y^+$ regions.
This phenomenon can also be implied by the lower Reynolds number.
The budget of the \acrshort{tke}, visualised in \autoref{fig:inflowSecVerifBudget}, give the same impression.
In this metrics, the three profiles were found to be highly consistent with one another and the reference data.
The statistics analysed so far demonstrate no noticeable differences between the plane and the curved \acrshort{tbl} simulations including both, the base and the optimised simulation setup of the R100deg30 case.
The last discussed quantity is the Clauser parameter $\beta$, represented in \autoref{fig:inflowSecVerifBeta}.
It is shown here only in the straight region of the domains, since this is the region of interest for this verification.
The streamwise coordinate is normalised by the reference boundary layer thickness $\delta_0$.
The trend of all simulations looks similar, meaning $\beta$ is constant near zero, except a small balancing region at the beginning of the domain.
Along the streamwise direction a slightly growing distance between the plane setup and the curved setups can be observed ending in a maximum distance at the end of the straight section of around $0.1$.
This trend could already show an impact of the curvature even in the upstream region.
However, all profiles show quite smooth trends of $\beta$ indicating low to negligible impact of the pressure fluctuations on the mean Clauser parameter.
Overall, the comparisons obtained show that in the straight region the simulations end up in similar profiles which leads to two mean conclusions.
First, it generally verifies the curved simulation setup in enabling an undisturbed \acrshort{tbl} development in the straight section.
And second, the results imply that the observed pressure fluctuations, discussed in XX show now visible manipulation of the statistics.



\section{Curved turbulent boundary layer results}

In the following section the results of the curved \acrshort{tbl} simulations are discussed.
First the results of each tested case are presented separately to directly compare the optimised configuration with the base configuration.
Then, the optimised configurations of all cases are compared to study the effect of different geometrical configurations on the optimisation and the development of the \acrshort{tbl}.

\subsection{R100deg 30}

This case is analysed with multiple quantities, to ensure a proper evolution of the \acrshort{tbl} along the curvature.
In the beginning we focus on the first and second order statistics extracted at $\mathrm{Re}_{\theta} = 1200$. 
This position is located inside the curvature, with some distance to both ends, to avoid interfering effects of the geometry changes.
Then, the growth of the boundary layer thickness and Reynolds numbers along the streamwise direction are presented. 
Here, we use as reference data from plane \acrshort{tbl} simulations performed by \citet{jimenez_near-wall_2013} extracted at $\mathrm{Re}_{\theta} = 1100$.
Finally, we will discuss the pressure-oriented parameters to evaluate the optimisation capability.

In \autoref{fig:R100deg30Re1200U} the velocity profile in viscous scales is depicted.
It can be seen that the base and optimised configuration show no difference in their velocity profiles.
Both also match the reference profile.
As already discussed in the previous shown velocity profiles, the only difference at the upper end of the boundary layer occurs because of the different extraction positions, respectively varying values of $\mathrm{Re}_{\theta}$. 
In addition, it can be seen that the profiles follow the analytical solutions of the \benennung{linear law} and the \benennung{log law}.
This indicates a correct transformation of the velocity components from fix $xyz$-coordinate system into streamwise and wall-normal components. 
Since the components in \name{neko} are defined in a fixed coordinate system, all quantities are transformed in the post-processing.
The correct implementation can be justified by the velocity profiles since in transformed directions the qualitative shape in wall-normal direction should not change in the curvature.
In \autoref{fig:R100deg30Re1200Fluct} the Reynolds stress components are depicted.
Similar to the velocity profiles, the differences between the optimised and the base configuration are negligible.
Also compared to the reference data only small variations in the outer region of the \acrshort{tbl} are visible.
As a last local statistics we focus on the budgets of the \acrshort{tke} in \autoref{fig:R100deg30Re1200Budget}.
It can be seen that the trends of the terms show no significant differences to the ones taken at $\mathrm{Re}_{\theta} = 700$ (see \autoref{sec:planeTBLVerif}).
The trends of all separate terms look quite similar compared to the ones in the straight section. 
In addition, both configurations show similar behaviour.
The analysed statistics allow some first suggestions about the \acrshort{tbl} behaviour in the curved section:
First, all profiles show a high qualitative accordance to the profiles in the straight part of this domain and respectively with the ones from the plane \acrshort{tbl}.
This could imply a small impact of the curvature to the local statistics at this exlpicit position.
However, this suggestion is very sensible to the domain geometry and the relative position of the extracted profile and thus cannot be generalised \citep{patel_longitudinal_1997}.
In the discussed case R100deg30 

To analyse the global evolution of the \acrshort{tbl}, the normalised boundary layer and momentum thicknesses are displayed in \autoref{fig:R100deg30Re1200Thickness}.
Their growth is clearly impacted by the curvature.
Up to $s/\delta_0 \approx 55$ both quantities constantly grow with increasing streamwise length.
Then, a sudden drop of both is remarkable. 
This position coincides with the start of the curvature in the domain which implies that the geometry change impacts the boundary layer growth.
After a small period in streamwise direction, the profiles recover and increase again. 
However, both quantities show a reduced slope such that their growth is decelerated in the curvature compared to the straight section.
This phenomenon is known and was already detected by XX.
in the curvature the growth rate stays constant until the end of the curvature is reached.
A sudden change of both quantities is remarkable at $s/\delta_0 \approx 110$ which marks the end of the curvature.
Reversely to the start of the curvature, here a sudden increase of the growth rate can be detected in all quantities.
Similar, this region is quite short in streamwise direction until the slope levels at a comparable niveau as in the curvature.
This observation forces the statement of XX that the curvature effects the growth of a boundary layer.
Besides this general behaviour for both configurations, some local differences between them can be detected.
In the straight part the configurations perfectly match each other. 
Right before the curvature starts, a small negative bump is visible for the optimised configuration.
The following drop of the thickness measures is similar in the configurations.
After the recovery of the drop, the profiles start to differ.
It can be seen that for both measures $\delta_{99}$ and $\theta$ the optimised profile develops with a higher growth rate than the base profile.
Additionally, in the optimised configuration the change of the growth rate due to the end of the curvature is smaller compared to the based profile.
However, the optimised configuration seems to be impacted earlier by the end of the curvature.

In \autoref{fig:R100deg30Re1200Prelated} the pressure related quantities are visualised.
Firstly, we focus on \autoref{fig:R100deg30Re1200Beta} where the Clauser parameter along the normalised streamwise coordinate is shown.
There, remarkable differences between the base and the optimised configuration are present.
In the first section, both configurations show a constant value of $\beta$ at zero which is already described in detail in \autoref{sec:planeTBLVerif}.
This part corresponds to the straight inlet of the domain.
At $s/\delta_0 \approx 55$ a sharp negative spike in both configurations can be detected.
This spike corresponds to the start of the curvature in the geometry. 
A similar spike in positive direction is visible at $s/\delta_0 \approx 120$, which marks the end of the curvature.
Here, it has to be noticed that the peak value for the optimised configuration is around two times the value of the base configuration.
In between the two spike, the two configurations show substantial differences in the evolution of the Clauser parameter.
In the base configuration, the Clauser parameter stays along the complete curvature smooth with a small constant decrease.
Contrary, the optimised configuration results in a fluctuating Clauser parameter in the curvature.
Along the curved section three positive bumps with an amplitude of $\Delta\beta \approx 0.2$.
A similar bump in negative direction is placed right before the curved section starts.
Upon initial observation, this behaviour seems to be counterintuitive, since the Clauser parameter represents the optimised quantity and therefore an improvement of $\beta$ in the optimised compared to the base configuration is expected.
To understand this behaviour in a wider context we first need to take two more plots into account.
In \autoref{fig:R100deg30Re1200p} the wall-pressure along the domain is depicted.
The streamwise direction is again normalised with the reference boundary layer thickness.
The pressure is visualised as a difference between the local pressure at the wall $p(s)$ and the mean pressure at the wall in the curved section of the domain $\langle p_c \rangle$.
With this kind of visualisation the pressure distribution along the streamwise direction can be analysed.
For purposes of simplification, the term 'pressure' or 'pressure distribution' is employed in the following analysis.
However, it should be noted that this refers to the visualised difference between the local and mean pressure in the curvature. 
In the first section, where the domain is straight, both configurations have a constantly decreasing pressure distribution with similar gradient.
They stay parallel to each other in the straight section, but with the optimised configuration having a lower absolute level.
Once the curvature starts, the behaviour of the two configurations start to differ significantly.
In the base configuration the start of the curvature is visible through a steep drop of the pressure level. 
After this drop, the pressure recovers such that it decreases further with a similar gradient as in front of the drop. 
A similar behaviour in the opposite direction can be seen at the end of the curvature.
There, a positive pressure jump can be detected.
Afterwards the pressure again decreases with a constant gradient.
Along the whole domain length a pressure decrease of $\Delta(p(s)-\langle p_c \rangle) \approx 0.09$ is visible.
The optimised configuration shows in the curved section a different trend.
Once the curvature starts, the pressure trend changes from decreasing to a constant level with small positive trend.
Small fluctuations in the curve are noticeable along the complete curved section.
However, the value stays constant at $\approx 0$ in the curvature. 
At the end of the curved section a steep increase of the pressure up to $0.04$ appears.
Near the end of the domain, again a slightly negative trend adjusts.
For a complete analysis we also have a look at the free-stream velocity in \autoref{fig:R100deg30Re1200Uinf}.
There the local free-stream velocity $U(s)$ relative to the free-stream velocity at the reference position $U_0$ is plotted along the normalised streamwise coordinate.
In both configurations a constant positive trend in the straight part is visible.
Similar to the previous described plot, the two configurations starts two differ once the curvature starts.
The base configuration shows a negative drop at the start of the curvature, then increases again with the same gradient as before.
At the end of the curvature the converse behaviour can be observed.
Overall, the base configuration shows a positive trend with an absolute difference of $\Delta \frac{U(s)}{U_0} \approx 0.08$.
In the optimised configuration, instead, a relatively positive spike occurs right before the curvature starts.
Afterwards the curve stops increasing in the curved section. 
Instead, a small negative trend can be seen, but with a lower absolute gradient than in the base configuration.
In addition, some fluctuations are visible in the curved section, similar as in \autoref{fig:R100deg30Re1200p}.
At the transition from curvature to the straight outlet a decrease in the velocity is visible which recovers until the end of the domain is reached.
Now the meaning of the presented plots is discussed.
In \autoref{fig:R100deg30Re1200Beta} we can observe that the optimisated configuration behaves converse the expectations.
Instead of an improved trend in terms of constancy at zero and smoothness, clearly higher fluctuations occur compared to the base configuration.
A closer look at the positions of the bumps, suggests a connection to the positioning of the \benennung{design points} in the optimisation setup.
As described in \autoref{subsec:optSetup} the optimisation is based on a finite number of \benennung{design points} whose position is adjusted to reduce the RMS of $\beta$.
This discrete \benennung{control points} are connected with a spline. 
The adjustment of one \benennung{control point} might lead to a modified pressure gradient in the cross-section where this \benennung{design point} is positioned.
Here, the pressure gradient is mentioned, since that is the field quantity that is actually impacted by a shape modification of the domain.
This positioning will change the size of the cross-sectional area will change, which impacts the surrounding area as well.
Due to mass conservation, this will change the local velocity and, thus, in the momentum balance cause a change in the local pressure gradient.
This behaviour is enforced, if the \benennung{control points} are distributed sparse along the arc because then the cross-sections in between the \benennung{control points} depend only on the spline shape chosen by the optimiser to connect them.
This could lead to relatively high changes of the cross-sectional area through unfavourable course of the spline.
A dense positioning of \benennung{control points} would prevent this behaviour.
Transferred to the discussed case, this implies a distribution of \benennung{control points} that is too sparse for a continuous control of the pressure-gradient ending up in visible fluctuations in the Clauser parameter.
However, \autoref{fig:R100deg30Re1200p} shows that the optimisation anyway affects the pressure field.
Here it can be seen, that the pressure distribution itself is 'improved' by the optimisation.
Compared to the base configuration a shift towards a more uniform pressure distribution is visible.
Subtracting the mean pressure in the curvature leads to an around zero pressure level meaning that along the whole curvature a more or less constant pressure occurs.
However, in the base configuration, the pressure decreases in the curved section similar as in the straight inlet.
Since the pressure declines with a constant slope, this constitution results in a constant pressure gradient.
Therefore, the optimisation affects the simulation setup such that the pressure itself shows a more constant distribution.
As an enforcing argument \autoref{fig:R100deg30Re1200Uinf} is considered.
The constantly increasing free-stream velocity in the base configuration is consistent with its decreasing pressure.
The decreasing pressure provokes a negative pressure-gradient, already introduced as \acrshort{fpg}.
This \acrshort{fpg} causes an acceleration of the free-stream which is visible in \autoref{fig:R100deg30Re1200Uinf}.
In contrast, the free-stream velocity in the optimised configuration shows a more constant trend on a global level.
Also this behaviour corresponds with the observed pressure distribution which tends to zero in the curved section.
Therefore, the global pressure gradient is smaller, thus resulting in a more uniform free-stream velocity.
However, on a local level also the free-stream profile of the optimised configuration shows fluctuations.
Since all three discussed plots show these fluctuations of similar constitution, it can be suggested that they occur for the same reason.
To ensure this, a detailed analysis of their positions in relation to the positions of the \benennung{control points} would be necessary.
Finally, the obtained results show, that even though the optimisation created a $\beta$-distribution with fluctuations inside the curved section, the pressure distribution is nonetheless shifted towards the desired, more uniform state on a global level.
