\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

\acrfull{cfd} is an application with rising importance for multiple engineering fields like aerospace, energy systems and process engineering \citep{versteeg_introduction_2007}.
The dramatic improvement of \acrshort{cfd} technology enables simulations of exceptional size and accuracy \citep{slotnick_cfd_2014}.
As a consequence, \acrshort{cfd} facilitates studies of applications where suitable experimental testings are impossible due to their physical size as e.g.~in aerospace applications \citep{slotnick_cfd_2014}.
In addition, it facilitates insights into underlying physical phenomena through a detailed resolution of the entire flow-field, providing access to quantities that are difficult or impossible to obtain experimentally \citep{versteeg_introduction_2007}.

This possibility is particularly important when phenomena of small physical scales are investigated.
In circumstances where a fluid comes into contact with a solid object, these small physical scale assume a pivotal role.
This situation occurs in all technical applications, where a fluid is limited by a wall, e.g.~fluid inside a pipe or around an airfoil \citep{marusic_wall-bounded_2010}.
In general, these flows are known as \benennung{wall-bounded flows}.
The processes near the wall are of special interest since they can be responsible for massive losses of energy in the flow \citep{fan_decomposition_2020}.
They are caused by the friction between the wall and the fluid itself which is determined by multiple factors as wall roughness, geometry, viscosity of the fluid \citep{schlichting_boundary-layer_2017}.
A reduction of these losses is important for
The range in which the flow is impacted by the interaction with the wall is usually limited in wall-normal direction.
This area is named \benennung{boundary layer}, first introduced by \citet{prandtl_uber_1904}.
In many applications the boundary layer is of turbulent characteristic, which impedes the study of its inner processes.
To simulate the turbulent structures inside a boundary layer, a high resolution in space and time is essential.
However, this goes along with high computational costs, which are unsuitable for commercial usage.
Consequently, simulation methods with such high accuracy are predominantly used in research settings.
With the so-called \acrfull{dns} all the turbulent scales can be resolved in the simulation to facilitate a full investigation of their interactions and relations.
In order to study the behaviour of \acrfull{tbl} under various conditions, \acrshort{dns} are vital. \\

Much research has been performed to obtain understanding of the underlying processes in \acrfull{tbl} along a plane wall \citep{pargal_adverse-pressure-gradient_2022}.
\citet{schlatter_turbulent_2012} studied the effect of inflow conditions and tripping on the development of \acrshort{tbl} along a flat-plate.
A detailed study of the structures in the \acrshort{tbl} was performed by \citet{jimenez_near-wall_2013}.
However, only a limited number of studies have concentrated on \acrshort{tbl} along curved surfaces, although this often happens in real-world applications \citep{spalart_direct_2024}.
\citet{muck_effect_1985} showed that the study of curvature effects are nonetheless important, since their investigations of convey and concave curvature showed fundamental differences in the \acrshort{tbl} development.
Also, \citet{chiwanga_effect_1993} made some interesting findings regarding the differences between turbulent structures along a flat wall and a convex curved wall.
Nevertheless, the investigation of curved \acrshort{tbl} holds some intricacies \citep{appelbaum_systematic_2025}.
\citet{so_experiment_1973} found in their experiments, that a curved geometry provokes a pressure gradient in the entire domain.
This pressure gradient also impacts the development of the \acrshort{tbl}.
Hence, the pressure induced changes in the \acrshort{tbl} interfere with the ones caused by the curved geometry.
In order to isolate the curvature induced effects, it is therefore necessary to manipulate the pressure conditions.
With this approach it is possible to achieve predictable pressure induced effects.
The natural choice of the prescribed pressure field has a constant value over the whole domain which results in a \acrfull{zpg} distribution.
This configuration is preferable since in \acrshort{tbl} along a flat wall this pressure distribution exists naturally and is therefore well-studied.
Since the pressure field cannot be set directly, an implicit approach is necessary to obtain the desired pressure distribution \citep{appelbaum_systematic_2025}.
Since this approach requires a non-trivial procedure to determine appropriate \acrfull{bc}, only a few researchers address the investigation of pressure isolated curved \acrshort{tbl}.
\citet{appelbaum_systematic_2025} tried to isolate curvature effects from pressure effects on a concave \acrshort{tbl}. \\

In this work a new procedure to create \acrshort{zpg} conditions in a curved \acrshort{tbl} domain is tested.
The approach is tested in three different curved geometries introduced in \autoref{sec:MethodCurvedTBL}.
The used approach is based on \citet{schlatter_leveraging_2025}.
The idea is to impose \acrshort{bc} constraining the velocity at the upper wall of the curved domain which modify the pressure field towards a \acrshort{zpg}.
Hence, these \acrshort{bc} for the velocity field need to be determined.
This is done with assistant simulations of lower accuracy. 
In these simulations the curved domain is simulated with a movable upper wall. 
The realisation of this setup is described in detail in \autoref{sec:OptimisationMethod}.
Through a number of movable control points the shape of the upper wall can be adjusted in order to obtain a \acrshort{zpg} at the lower wall.
This procedure is implemented in an optimisation algorithm introduced by \citet{morita_applying_2022}.
The algorithm optimises the shape of the upper wall to obtain the prescribed target value of the \benennung{Clauser parameter} $\beta$, which is a non-dimensionalised variable for the pressure gradient, along the lower wall.
From the optimised configuration, we extract the velocity profile at a specific wall-normal height.
The extracted velocity profile, is then imposed as \acrshort{bc} in a new fully resolved simulation.
This boundary condition should then induce a similar pressure field as in the optimised simulation, respectively with a \acrshort{zpg} at the lower wall.
In this simulation, then the effects of the curvature on the \acrshort{tbl} can be studied.
The aim of this work is to employ this procedure to the three investigated curved geometries and evaluate the capability of the optimisation algorithm in each case.
In addition, the simulations with the manipulated velocity \acrshort{bc} are studied to extract information about the effect of curvature on the \acrshort{tbl}.
Here, also the differences between the different geometries are of special interest.\\

In order to present the obtained findings in this work, first the theoretical fundamentals are introduced in \autoref{chap:basics}.
Following, the employed methods and simulations setups are presented in \autoref{chap:methods}.
In \autoref{chap:validation} the validation studies are analysed followed by the discussion of results in \autoref{chap:results}.
Finally, the governed findings are summarised and an outlook for future investigations is given.
